How to Notarize a Power of Attorney: Step-by-Step Guide

A practical guide to preparing a POA, checking witness and state requirements, and completing an in-person or remote notarization.

A power of attorney (POA) is a document that authorizes one person—the agent or attorney-in-fact—to act for another person, called the principal. Depending on the document and state law, a POA may need notarization, witnesses, or both before it can be used.

Before you sign: Ask the bank, healthcare provider, title company, or other organization that will receive the POA what form and signing requirements it accepts. Notarizing a document does not fix missing legal language or automatically make it valid.

Can you notarize a power of attorney online?

Remote online notarization may be available when the notary is authorized to perform it, the signer meets identity and technology requirements, and the POA is eligible under applicable law. Some states, institutions, or document types impose special rules. Verify acceptance before choosing an online appointment.

How to get a power of attorney notarized

  1. Get the correct POA form. Use a form appropriate for your state and purpose. A financial POA, healthcare POA, and limited POA may have different requirements.
  2. Complete the document carefully. Enter names and information as instructed. Do not sign or date it early if the form requires signing in front of a notary or witnesses.
  3. Confirm witnesses. Some POAs require one or more witnesses in addition to notarization. Ask whether witnesses must be disinterested or meet other criteria.
  4. Prepare identification. Online notary platforms typically ask for an accepted government-issued photo ID and identity verification. Check the provider's accepted ID list in advance.
  5. Choose an in-person or remote notary. Confirm that the receiving organization accepts the chosen notarization method.
  6. Sign during the required process. Follow the notary's instructions. Do not sign in advance unless the notary and document instructions permit it.
  7. Review and store the completed POA. Confirm the notarial certificate is complete and provide copies to the agent and relevant institutions as appropriate.

What do you need to bring?

  • The completed but unsigned POA, if required by the signing instructions
  • Valid government-issued photo identification accepted by the notary
  • Any required witnesses
  • Instructions from the receiving bank, healthcare provider, title company, or other institution
  • Any supporting documents requested by the receiving organization

Important: notarization is not the same as creating a POA

A notary does not decide what powers your agent receives, whether the POA is durable, when it takes effect, or whether it meets every legal requirement. Those issues depend on the document and applicable law. If you need help choosing or drafting a POA, consider consulting a qualified attorney or an appropriate legal aid resource.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Using a generic form that does not meet your state's requirements
  • Signing before the required witnesses or notary are present
  • Assuming remote notarization is accepted by every institution
  • Leaving required fields blank or using inconsistent names
  • Failing to confirm whether the POA must be recorded or delivered to a specific office

Frequently asked questions

Does a power of attorney need to be notarized?

Many POA forms require notarization, witnesses, or both. Requirements vary by state and type of POA; check the form instructions and receiving institution.

Can I notarize a POA online?

Possibly. Confirm that remote notarization is authorized for the notary and document, and that the institution receiving the POA accepts it.

Can I sign my POA before the notary appointment?

Do not sign early unless the document instructions and notary confirm that this is permitted. Many documents require signing or acknowledgment during the notarization.

Does a notary make my power of attorney legally valid?

Not by itself. Notarization is one formal step; document content, witnesses, capacity, execution rules, and other legal requirements may also matter
